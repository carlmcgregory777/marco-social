SHOT SIZES
Start with the frame
Shot size controls the balance between the person, their actions and the surrounding place.
Extreme wide / EWS
 The environment takes priority and the
person appears very small. This scale
can make a place feel vast, or a
character seem isolated within it. Use
it when geography or distance matters
more than facial expression. Make the
figure easy to find through placement
or contrast.
PROMPT
Extreme wide shot; keep the subject small within the surrounding environment.
Wide / WS or LS
 The whole person is visible with
generous space around them. We can
read where they are, what surrounds
them and where they might move
next. Useful for entrances, movement
and relationships across a location.
Keep enough space for the action
without making the subject disappear.
PROMPT
Wide shot; full figure with open space around them.

01
SHOT SIZES
Full shot / FS
The person fills much of the frame
from head to feet. Posture, clothing
and the complete physical action
become the main sources of
information. Useful for introducing a
character or showing a stance, walk
or dance. Keep the head and feet
inside the composition.
PROMPT
Full-body shot; keep the head and feet inside the frame.
Medium long / MLS
 A knee-up view keeps the face,
hands and much of the body
together. It gives a gesture more
space than a waist-up shot while
bringing us closer than a full figure.
Also called a medium wide shot. Use
it for standing dialogue, a change of
posture or an action involving the
hands and legs.
PROMPT
Medium long shot; frame the person from head to knees.

01
SHOT SIZES
Cowboy / mid-thigh
The frame runs from the head to
around mid-thigh. It keeps the hands
and hip area visible while making the
face more prominent than in a full
shot. The name comes from
Westerns, where this framing
included a gun holster. It also works
for a reach, a standoff or confident
body language.
PROMPT
Cowboy shot; head to mid-thigh, hands visible.
Medium / MS
 A waist-up view balances expression
with upper-body movement. The
viewer can follow a conversation and
notice what the person is doing with
their hands. A useful starting point for
dialogue and simple actions. If an
important gesture falls outside the
image, make the framing wider.
PROMPT
Medium shot; waist-up, camera at eye level.

01
SHOT SIZES
Medium close-up / MCU
The head, shoulders and upper chest
fill the image. Expression becomes
the priority, but a lean, breath or turn
of the shoulders still has room to
register. Useful for dialogue and
reactions that need intimacy without
the intensity of a tight facial close-up.
Keep the background from
competing with the eyes.
PROMPT
Medium close-up; head and shoulders, chest at the lower edge.
Close-up / CU
 The face dominates and most
surroundings fall away. Small
changes in the eyes and mouth
become readable, making this useful
for a reaction or a moment of
recognition. Choose the expression
that carries the scene. A close-up has
more impact when the preceding
wider view has already established
what the person is reacting to.
PROMPT
Close-up of the face; blurred background.

SHOT SIZES
Extreme close-up / ECU
An extreme close-up fills the frame with one small feature: an eye, a fingertip or a crucial part of an object.
It directs attention to information that could disappear in a wider view.
Use it to reveal a clue, a tiny physical reaction or a surface detail. Choose exactly what the viewer needs to
notice: an eye tightening, a finger pressing a button, or a damaged edge.
PROMPT
Extreme close-up of the key detail; exclude the surrounding context.
Size is not lens choice. "Close-up" describes the framing. Focal length and camera distance are separate
decisions. For a first image test, state the frame boundary before adding lens language.

02
CAMERA ANGLES
Change the viewpoint
Keep the shot size in mind while moving the camera above or below the subject.
Eye level
At the subject’s eye height, the camera feels like a direct
encounter. This is a useful baseline for dialogue:
expression and behaviour can lead without an obvious
view from above or below.
PROMPT
Eye-level camera; lens at the subject’s eye height.
High angle
Looking down reveals more ground and can make a
person seem smaller or exposed within the space. Use it
to clarify position or suggest vulnerability; the scene
supplies the emotional meaning.
PROMPT
Camera above the subject, looking down.
Low angle
Looking upward can make a figure or building seem
imposing. The ground gives way to sky or ceiling, and
nearby features may appear larger. Useful when height or
presence matters.
PROMPT
Low camera position, looking upward.

02
CAMERA ANGLES
Overhead and off-level
Camera angle and camera movement are separate choices.
Overhead / top-down
 The camera looks almost vertically
down. The image becomes a map of
positions, routes and patterns, with
less emphasis on faces and more on
how elements occupy the space.
Useful for choreography, object
arrangements or revealing how a
place is organised. An overhead view
can be static; it does not imply a flying
camera.
PROMPT
Directly overhead; camera looking straight down.
Dutch angle
 The camera is rolled off level, so the
horizon and normally upright lines
become diagonal. That imbalance can
support unease, confusion or an
unstable point of view. Choose the
slant deliberately. Holding it gives a
Dutch angle; changing the slant during
the shot creates a camera roll.
PROMPT
Dutch angle; a clearly diagonal horizon.

CAMERA MOVEMENTS
Pan: turn on the spot
The camera rotates horizontally from a fixed position.
A / START
B / END
A pan can follow action, connect two parts of a location or delay a reveal. A slow turn gives the viewer time
to explore; a fast one can redirect attention abruptly. Choose a direction and a stopping point. The camera
pivots in place; moving it sideways would be a truck move.
PROMPT
Pan right at an even pace from a fixed position; keep the horizon level and settle on the new view.

Tilt: look up or down
The camera pivots vertically while its position stays fixed.
A / START
B / END
A tilt reveals a subject vertically: from an object to its owner, or from a building entrance to its roof. The
change can emphasise height or let information arrive gradually. The lens changes direction; the camera
does not rise. Use a pedestal or boom when the viewpoint itself needs to become higher.
PROMPT
Tilt smoothly upward from a fixed position; keep the camera at the same height and stop on the
upper subject.

CAMERA MOVEMENTS
Dolly in and dolly out
The camera travels towards or away from the subject.
A / CLOSER
B / FARTHER
A push-in can concentrate attention on a thought or reaction. A pull-out gives the surroundings more
weight, revealing context or leaving the person isolated within a larger space. Read B to A for a push-in, or
A to B for a pull-out. Physical travel changes the relationship between foreground and background; a zoom
keeps the viewpoint fixed.
PROMPT
Dolly slowly forward to a closer view; keep focal length and camera height fixed, with the subject
near centre.

CAMERA MOVEMENTS
Truck and tracking
Travel through the scene while deciding what stays steady in the frame.
Truck: move sideways
A / OBSTRUCTED VIEW
B / REVEAL
A truck moves the camera sideways. Nearby objects shift farther across the frame than the distant
background. This parallax can reveal a subject or open a new view without moving closer.
Track: travel with the subject
A / START
B / FOLLOW THE SUBJECT
A tracking shot travels through the scene, often alongside a moving person. Matching their pace keeps us
with the action while the passing background makes the route and distance felt.
PROMPT
Track parallel to the moving subject at a matching pace; keep a steady distance and consistent
framing.

CAMERA MOVEMENTS
Arc: travel around
The camera follows a curved path around a subject.
A / SIDE VIEW
B / FRONTAL VIEW
Travelling around a subject changes both the visible side and the background behind it. Use this to reveal
spatial relationships or give a still moment a sense of energy. Keep the subject stationary in a first test. The
camera changes viewpoint along the curve; the subject does not turn to create the new angle.
PROMPT
Arc from a side view to a frontal view at a steady distance; the subject stays still.

CAMERA MOVEMENTS
Zoom: change the field of view
The camera stays in place while the lens changes the framing.
A / WIDER FIELD
B / TIGHTER FIELD
A zoom narrows or widens the field of view. It can direct attention to a detail while preserving the
viewpoint; foreground and background enlarge together. Choose a dolly when you want travel through
space. Choose a zoom when you want the framing to tighten or widen from the same position.
PROMPT
Increase focal length smoothly from a fixed camera position; finish on a tighter frame of the
subject.

CAMERA MOVEMENTS
Dolly zoom: balance two
changes
Camera travel and an opposite zoom keep the subject approximately the same size.
A / CLOSE + WIDE LENS
B / FAR + LONG LENS
The subject stays about the same size while the background expands or recedes. This unsettling change
can give a moment of shock or sudden realisation a visible spatial effect. In A to B, the camera moves back
and zooms in. The foreground subject stays similarly sized while the background grows. Test both
changes together in motion to keep the framing steady.
PROMPT
Move the camera backwards while zooming in; keep the subject the same size in frame.

CAMERA MOVEMENTS
Camera handling and speed
Choose a stable frame, a physical camera feel or a deliberate burst of movement.
Static
Lock the viewpoint. The action can change while the frame
stays steady.
PROMPT
Locked-off camera; keep the composition fixed while
the action unfolds.
Handheld
Small variations in position and angle give the shot a
physical, responsive feel.
PROMPT
Restrained handheld drift; keep the subject clearly
framed.
Roll
Rotate around the lens axis so the horizon turns. Holding that
slant creates a Dutch angle.
PROMPT
Slow clockwise camera roll from a level frame.
Whip pan
A fast horizontal turn blurs the space between two views,
redirecting attention abruptly.
PROMPT
Whip pan right with brief motion blur; settle on a
sharp, readable final frame.

PRACTICE
Put the camera into words
Start with framing. Add the angle. Describe movement only when planning video.
For a still image
PROMPT
Medium close-up, eye-level camera. Keep the main subject clearly separated from the background.
The camera clause does most of the work. The subject and setting give the model somewhere to apply it.
Inspect the returned frame before adding more detail.
For a moving shot
PROMPT
Begin with a medium shot. Slowly dolly in to a close-up, keeping the subject in the same frame
position.
Specify the path, pace, what stays steady and the final composition. For a first test, change one camera
choice at a time: this makes an unwanted pan, zoom or drift easier to diagnose.
Try a three-shot sequence

02
03
Wide: establish the subject and the surrounding space.
Medium: make a small gesture readable.
Close-up: finish on the reaction.
